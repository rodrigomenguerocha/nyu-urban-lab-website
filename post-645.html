The New York Times: Electric Grids Are a Hidden Weak Spot in World’s Climate Plans, Report Warns

November 20, 2023

Climate ChangeImpact Development NewsRenewable EnergySustainable DevelopmentSustainable Infrastructure


The International Energy Agency's recent analysis has shed light on a concerning trend: as clean energy technologies like solar panels, wind turbines, and electric vehicles gain global traction, many countries are falling behind in developing the necessary power lines and electric grids to support them. According to the report, an estimated 50 million miles of power lines need to be built or upgraded by 2040 to meet renewable energy goals and transition to electric vehicles and heating. This colossal task involves nearly doubling the size of the world's existing electric grids in just two decades.

The challenge becomes even more pronounced when considering the need to double investments in transmission lines and infrastructure, reaching $600 billion per year by 2030. However, with the exception of China, many countries have seen a decline in investments in grid development. This oversight is likened to focusing on building high-performance renewable power plants without giving equal attention to the necessary grid infrastructure.

The significance of electric grids becomes apparent when considering the role they play in connecting remote regions with abundant wind and solar resources to urban centers. With wind and solar expected to contribute 80% of new electric capacity by 2040, the lack of grid infrastructure is a bottleneck for these clean energy sources. Globally, there are vast amounts of renewable energy awaiting connection to power lines, equivalent to five times the amount of solar and wind installed in the previous year.

The challenges include lengthy wait times for new projects to connect to the grid, with delays of up to five years in the United States. Public opposition and legal battles further complicate the construction of new power lines, as seen in projects like the Südlink transmission project in Germany. Upgrading local infrastructure in cities to accommodate increased power demand from electric vehicle charging stations also poses a time-consuming challenge.

Extreme weather events, exacerbated by global warming, add another layer of complexity, increasing the risk of blackouts and necessitating additional investments to fortify electric grids. Each country faces unique challenges in grid development, ranging from financial health concerns of local utilities in certain regions to high borrowing costs in sub-Saharan Africa.

While technology can offer solutions, such as more efficient use of existing grid infrastructure and innovative approaches to managing power flow, the report underscores the urgent need for substantial and immediate attention to grid development. Failure to expand grids rapidly could have severe consequences for climate change, leading to higher carbon emissions and hindering the transition to cleaner energy sources.
