Housing in Brief: Only 5.8% of L.A.’s Emergency Housing Vouchers Were Used

July 28, 2024

Affordable HousingCase StudiesIncentives &amp; ToolsNational Trends

This article discusses challenges in distributing housing vouchers in Los Angeles, the Fed’s role in the housing crisis, and potential San Francisco affordable housing initiatives. The city of Los Angeles faced significant challenges in effectively utilizing emergency housing vouchers distributed through the American Rescue Plan. A report by the L.A. Times revealed that only 5.8% of the 3,365 vouchers allocated were successfully used. The Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles (HACLA) mentioned understaffing, delays and a limited housing supply as key factors. However, in contrast to Los Angeles struggles, nearby Santa Barbara utilized all its vouchers. The City of Los Angeles had more success distributing other vouchers. Their Housing Choice Vouchers allocation, also called Section 8 vouchers, are allotted by the federal government and distributed by local housing authorities. A report from 2016-2020 displayed more vouchers being used and more permanent placements happening. 

The series of interest rate hikes by the Federal Reserve impacted the housing market significantly. Mortgage rates, around 5.5% for 30-year loans according to Freddie Mac, made home purchases less affordable for many prospective buyers, prompting them to remain in the rental market instead. Despite this, the housing market was and is facing a persistent shortage, with an estimated need for 7 million homes to meet demand. Analysts at Morgan Stanley anticipated that housing prices may continue to rise due to this supply-demand imbalance exacerbated by higher construction costs amid rising interest rates. Subsequently, higher mortgage rates made home ownership less accessible for individuals while facilitating easier acquisitions by corporate entities, particularly private equity firms. These firms have increasingly purchased single-family homes for rental conversions, and exacerbated rental market competition and driving up rents. 

In San Francisco, two competing ballot measures were debated highlighting divergent housing strategies in the city. Supervisor Connie Chan's proposal aimed for 29.5% of units in mixed-income buildings to be affordable, while Mayor London Breed's "Affordable Homes Now" measure suggested 21.5% affordable units. Breed's measure sought to remove supervisors' authority to block spending on affordable housing projects funded through city borrowing, whereas Chan's measure retained this oversight. These measures reflect broader tensions between YIMBY advocates pushing for increased housing supply and tenant-oriented groups advocating for more stringent affordability requirements. Critics argued that high affordable housing mandates could stifle new construction, while others highlighted the city's current overachievement in market-rate housing compared to state-mandated affordable units.
